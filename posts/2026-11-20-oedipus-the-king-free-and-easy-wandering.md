## Sophocles, *Oedipus the King*

### Knowledge

> Oedipus is famous for being intelligent: he solved the riddle of the Sphinx and saved Thebes.  
> However, throughout the play, he also misunderstands important things happening around him.  
> What is the difference between being intelligent and truly knowing?  
> Choose one moment when Oedipus is confident that he understands a situation but his understanding is incomplete.  

Being intelligent is like having a good race car, and in that sense, truly knowing is being at the finish line. Having a good vehicle helps, but isn't required to reach the finish line nor is guaranteed. In the case of Oedipus, it felt to me he was in denial. After the messenger told him he wasn't Polybus and Merope's son, he should have been able to put two and two together, as did Jocasta, and he might have (subconsciously), but kept telling himself there's a chance something that terrible did not happen.

### Seeing and Knowing

> Teiresias is physically blind, while Oedipus can physically see.  
> Yet their confrontation repeatedly connects sight, blindness, knowledge, and ignorance.  
> Who really “sees” more clearly in this scene?  
> Use specific words or lines from the play to explain your answer.  

If "sees" here refers to knowledge - knowing something that isn't put right in front of your face, then obviously Teiresias wins. Teiresias is a metahuman with the power of clairvoyance and/or precognition, something Oedipus did not have.

> "I say you are the murderer of the king whose murderer you seek."  
> — Teiresias, line 395, showing his power of clairvoyance, that allows him to know something he shouldn't have been able to know.

### Dramatic Irony

> Find one moment when Oedipus says or does something that the audience understands differently because we know more than he does.  
> Explain:  
> What does Oedipus think is happening?  
> What does the audience understand that he does not?  
> Why does this difference matter?  

~Line 1110 - 1150
When Oedipus didn't manage to put two and two together to figure out he was Jocasta's son. He kept digging to find out the truth about his birth. This is less of a case where the audience knows more because they were given access to information that the characters didn't have access to (as Jocasta was able to successfully deduce the information), and feels more to me like Oedipus was either deluding himself or suffering from tunnel vision. Usually when the character acts rationally using the information available to them, but inoptimal in the broader sense as the information is incomplete, it might create a sense of dread - like when you know something bad is going to happen but is powerless to stop it. But in the case of when the same information available to the audience are available to the characters, but the characters make irrational decisions due to a character flaw, it might shift the feeling into frustration.

## Zhuangzi, "Free and Easy Wandering"

### The Peng and Perspective

> The cicada, dove, and little quail cannot understand the enormous Peng or its journey.  
> Are the small creatures simply wrong, or are they judging the Peng according to the limits of their own experience?  
> What does this episode suggest about perspective?  

This is not an "or" question, as the two options aren't mutually exlusive. They were possibly doing both. The resolution they came to was incorrect, but they weren't necessary irrational in their way of thinking. Much like what happens when you tell a person that there's someone in New York who is able to fly, lift a building and shoot lasers out of their eyes, their response will (and should) be that you are full of shit unless they can confirm it with trusted sources or see it for themselves, as that is pretty much physically impossible with our current understanding of the world.

The text though, does not make clear whether the trio did see Peng themselves, or merely heard the story from somewhere else. That missing detail can completely changes whether their deduction is rational or not. Nevertheless, in real life, there are many humans whose abilities in certain fields have reached a level that feels impossible to the average person.

The answer to what the episode suggests lies in the next question:

### "Little Understanding" and "Great Understanding"

> The text says:  
> “Little understanding cannot come up to great understanding.”  
> Using one example from the selection, explain what you think this means.  

The example is in the previous question (The trio).

Basically, this can be intepreted as "Sometimes, the things you think are impossible are actually aren't, and you only think so because it feels impossible to you". 

> How can our own experience limit what we are able to imagine or understand?  

We judge things based on our experiences and knowledge. You modify the parameters, the result changes. As above, if your experiences have only shown you things within a certain level, then extreme outliers beyond that range of possibilites within what your experiences have conditioned you to expect will feel hard to believe. Only way to solve this is to broaden your horizons.

### Freedom and Dependence

> The Peng can fly an extraordinary distance, but it depends on a powerful wind.  
> Liezi can ride the wind, but he also still “had to depend on something.”  
> Can someone or something be truly free while still depending on external conditions?  
> What kind of freedom might “free and easy wandering” suggest?  

The Peng transformed from a fish into what is seemingly a powerful phoenix. Completely nonsensical.
Liezi is a metahuman with the ability to manipulate wind, that allows him to fly around the earth. Also completely nonsensical.
Zhuangzi suggests Huizi didn't think big enough, and gave an actually pretty decent advice. Not nonsensical.

What these might have in common suggests the freedom comes from not being bound to certain expectations, no matter how sensible or nonsensical it might be. Because who knows - that nonsensical thing you think of? Might not actually be nonsensical at all.

### "Free and Easy Wandering"

> Have you ever judged a person, choice, object, activity, or way of life as strange, impractical, unsuccessful, or “useless” because it did not fit your own expectations?  

The trio heard of Peng and his feats, and decided that it was all nonsense. Back when I used to play videogames a bunch, I had a period where I suspected nearly every single person who manages to top me of being a cheater. Either I tell myself their crosshair placement is weird, or their movement is weird, or their decision making is weird. That obviously wasn't the case. The more I praciced, the more I learned, the more I realized what I believed was to be too difficult or impossible to do, are actually pretty mediocre, and that my own inexperience deluded me into thinking what I did was the norm, and what deviated from that must be a cheat's quirk.
