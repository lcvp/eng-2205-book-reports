# Extra Credit: Amrum

I was initially looking forward to [Pickpocket][pickpocket], but the tickets sold out instantly for that one, so this is what I went for. I don't remember what the available seats were like, and that combined with the fact that I didn't know how the seat numbering works, led me to buy literally the worst seat in the entire room.

![seat-view]

It was so close to the screen, it distorted my view of the image and I had to look to the left for the entire duration of the movie. Would not recommend. Don't make the same mistakes I did.

![amrum-poster]

Amrum takes place on an island of the same name in Germany, set near the end of the second World War. The story follows a boy whose overarching goal in the entire movie was to get a piece of white bread with honey and butter for his Nazi mother. The 90 minutes of screentime centers around this goal: He gradually manages to obtain each ingrediences through whatever way he could, and on the way the audience gets to see and observe to the lives of ordinary people at the time through each of his interactions.

Unlike many movies which I (and most casual, modern audiences) usually watch, the movie does not follow the typical Three-act structure and blockbuster formulas. There was no plot. There was no twist. There was no central conflict. No shock, gore, nor violence like what you would expect from a "war" movie. The entire movie is just showing the days in life of a teenage boy and the people around him. The only movie I've ever watch that's similar to this one is [Perfect Days][perfect-days] - a slice of life that centers around a toilet cleaner in Japan and his the experiences in his mundane life.

These kinds of movies, for me, work like palate cleansers in between all those dopamine-filled Hollywood heavy hitters. They aren't for everybody. They are a bit slow. You would hate it if you approach it, expecting it to be something it isn't. It's like a nice cup of tea on a tranquil day: sort of an acquired taste, though one I have yet to have sufficient exposure in.

For the movie itself, there isn't much I have to talk about. The protagonist - Nanning - isn't your Gary Stu that is perfect and likable in everything. He is merely your average teenage boy, and a relatable one at that. But otherwise, it unfortunately did not leave a strong impression on me, unlike Perfect Days, even though they were in kind of in the same vein. With Amrum, it doesn't feel to have anything new enough to warrant a discussion for me. Usually topics that you would expect to reflect in these reports about these "war" movies are topics like horrors of war and all that jazz. But unlike many other "war" movies, Amrum merely uses war as a backdrop: the central thing it seems to want the audience to experience is to have a look into the mundane life of everyday people back then.
