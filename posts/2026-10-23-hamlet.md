# Hamlet

Date: 23/Oct/2026  
Length: 101 pages.

> "I heard thee speak me a speech once, but it was never acted; or, if it was, not above once; for the play, I remember, pleased not the million; 'twas caviare to the general: ..."  
> — **Hamlet**, *Act 2.2 394-395*

Insanely apt meta commentary of my situation. Much like what Hamlet said, Hamlet (the play) feels pretty much like caviar to me.  
Something of high culture that can't be fully appreciated by the general public. If I remember correctly, I might have tried it once and found it to be disguisting.  
Unlike caviar though, whose entire value derives from its artificial economic scarcity rather than genuine merit, Shakespeare's Hamlet must have been highly intellectually stimulating for those who are able to appreciate it, for it to gain such a foothold as one of the pillars of World Literature.  
Unfortunately, I am not one of those people.

I thought [Gilgamesh][the-epic-of-gilgamesh-blog-post] was weird. Oh boy, little did I know what was laying ahead of me.

## What is Hamlet

In short, Hamlet is one of the most popular literature works in the world, written by a guy whose name is pretty much synonymous with literature itself (Shakespeare).  
It tells the story of the titular prince, whose uncle murdered his father then takes the throne, and his story of revenge, filled with twists and turns and personal conflicts.

At the time of this writing, I couldn't finish reading the play. Only managed to get through half the text while barely understanding anything.  
The below is my summary of what I did managed to get through.

- Act 1
    - Scene 1: The soldiers talk about the presence of a ghost that looks like the late King and the fate of the country? (possibly)
    - Scene 2: Hamlet's uncle and his mother gets married. Mother discourages Hamlet from grieving for his dead father. The soldiers fron Scene 1 informs Hamlet of the ghost.
    - Scene 3: Laertes possibly bad mouths Hamlet, until Polonius enters the room and tells him to avoid judging people. Ophelia tells Polonius Hamlet is interested in her, and Polonius tells her to take his word with a graind of salt.
    - Scene 4: Hamlet and the soldiers watch upon their (guard platform?) and sees celebration until the ghost appears. Hamlet wants to follow it alone but the guards deter him. Follows anyway.
    - Scene 5: The ghost reveals he is Hamlet's father, who was murdered by the current king. The ghost encourages Hamlet to take revenge. Hamlet and the ghost make the soldiers swear not to tell anyone. 
- Act 2
    - Scene 1: Polonius tells Reynaldo something (I was unable to decode their conversation). Ophelia tells Polonius Hamlet was tripping out in her room. Polonius thinks Hamlet went mad with unrequited love (Polonius told Ophelia to not trust Hamlet earlier).
        - Note: The wikipedia page mentions something about Fortinbras were ordered by Norway to abandon an invasion, but I still am unable to see how the conversation indicates that.
    - Scene 2: The King and the Queen orders Rosencrantz and Guildenstern to check in on Hamlet while feigning ignorance. Polonius tells the King and Queen of his suspicion regarding Hamlet's recent erratic behavior. Hamlet manages to sniff out Rosencrants and Guildenstern's are up to, but make them promise to keep quiet. Hamlet hires a bunch of actors to play a play that might help him confirm the King's guilt.

## Hell on earth

### Arcane

Gilgamesh was weird. I didn't "get" it. As in I don't understand what the author(s) was trying to achive.  
But I did atleast understand, on a superficial level, the meaning of every single line.

Hamlet though? Saying my comprehension completely breaks down would be an understatement.  
Shakespeare is verbose. He loves having his characters going on and on about how one thing is the most <adjective>, <adjective>, <adjective>.  
Or how Adjectively adjective <ancient english word that I'm not even sure if is an adjective or a noun> adverb adjective.  
Not that there's anything wrong with that, in the literary sense.  
It's probably a good thing even. It's one of the most popular literature works in the world after all.  

The problem is, I don't understand half of the words he used. It's this blend of being just understandable enough for me to *get* the very general gist (heavy emphasis on very here) of the conversation but foreign enough to not understand what the hell a sentence is even trying to convey.  

So for huge chunks of dialogue, even when I don't understand what the hell the characters are saying, I have to keep push forward entire pages to *try* to deduce what the conversation could be about, using context clues and the very few words that I'm actually familiar with.  

Then go back, with the new information, try to piece together the pieces. Repeat 3-4 times.  

It got so bad the footnotes barely even serve any purpose. Like it doesn't matter if they clarify a word or two in a paragraph, because that paragraph also have 10 other words I don't understand.

Unsustainable isn't even close to describing it.

So I had to take to the internet for help. Which better place would be more fitting than reddit?

### The plot isn't the point.

The solution is simple. The english is weird, so I should probably find a translation with modern, natural english that I can understand.

Except the redditors have collectively agreed that doing that defeats the purpose. According to them, the plot is *alledgedly* pretty mediocre, and the thing that make the text special, is now Shakespeare manages to weave together the words like a pianist.  

Which is why I decided to find a play to watch.

### To see or not to see

> In my experience, the difference between a good Shakespeare performance and a bad one is the difference between understanding it and not. The act of reading it in high school, without seeing it performed (well), leaves you with almost nothing.  
>   
> Even if you can appreciate the poetry of it, a good Shakespeare performance can be transcendental.  
> — **FaultyLoom67**, *Reddit*

Hamlet was written to be played. Not to be read.  
But each play can differs so much in interpretation.  
The quality of the play can so drastically alters the perception of the work, yet it was still so popular, it must mean the real substance really lies on the text, is it not?  

Watching a bad performance is like eating food made from the finest ingredients but cooked by a shitty chef.

And after scouring around the corners of the internet, I settled with BBC's 2018 rendition of Hamlet featuring Andrew Scott. Mostly because his performance was glazed to hell on reddit, particularly his delivery on the "To be or not to be" monologue. And the commenters say it can't go wrong.

#### It went wrong

The play was not what I imagined. I expected something a little more authentic, a little more "true" to the period it was written in, like in a medieval setting of sorts. Instead, they adapted it to a modern setting, with phones and screens and guns and people in expensive suits. Threw me off a little bit, but I figured I might enjoy it if I just push through.

I did not.

#### Adaptation

The play switched around some scenes. So I couldn't simply follow along with my text copy, I have to read ahead first, then watch until I reaches a segment that I couldn't recognize.

But that wasn't the thing that gave me the most trouble.

> "Madam, I swear I use no art at all.  
> That he is mad, 'tis true: 'tis true 'tis pity,  
> And pity 'tis 'tis true: a foolish figure;  
> But farewell it, for I will use no art.  
> Mad let us grant him then: and now remains  
> That we find out the cause of this effect,  
> Or rather say, the cause of this defect,  
> For this effect defective comes by cause:  
> Thus it remains and the remainder thus.  
> Perpend.  
> — Polonius, Act 2.2 96-105

Hamlet (the script) does **not** read like natural conversation. It reads like a bunch of poets doing over the top deliveries, sort of like [those corporate androids in Severance (Mr Milkshake)][severance-conversation] but in a more *artsy* manner, like that song [Soldier, Poet, King][soldier-poet-king].

This actors in this play does not do that, they talked like normal human beings having normal conversations, just sometimes with big words sprinkled in. No different than how British royalties speak in common media to me.

Really wished I had chosen a play that is more in tune with my taste instead.

#### Say the line, Bart

> "To be, or not to be: that is the question:  
> Whether 'tis nobler in the mind to suffer  
> The slings and arrows of outrages fortune,  
> Or to take arms against a sea of troubles,  
> And by opposing end them. To die: to sleep;  
> No more; and by a sleep to say we end  
> The heart-ache, and the thousand natural shocks  
> That flesh is heir to, 'tis a consummation  
> Devoutely to be wished. To die, to sleep; ...  
> — Hamlet, Act 3.1 56-64

The famous monologue that seemingly everyone zeroes in on.  
One of the biggest reason why I chose the 2018 version of the play. Because Andrew Scott's performance of the monologue was so incredible, everyone online glazes it to hell.

And it didn't make me feel any different than the last 50 pages that I read through.  
The reason was simple. I didn't understand it.

## Death to Kronos

The text is brutal. Made me feel exactly like back when my IELTS score was 6.5. Had to skim around, look for keywords, analyze and deduce information as if it I was doing a math problem, instead of being able to immerse myself with the words, the world and the characters. Instead of being able to feel like a pianist skating around the keys with my fingers, it felt like I was [Dr. Strange getting all his fingers broken after a car crash][dr-strange-broken-fingers].

A week absolutely was **not** enough for me to be able to appreciate this work. Not even remotely close. Hell, it wasn't even enough for me to remotely understand it. Not when I have to use 100% of my brain power to wrestle with the words instead of simply taking it in and see where it take me.
