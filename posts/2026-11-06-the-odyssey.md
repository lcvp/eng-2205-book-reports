### The Greek performance

I was not impressed with the Ancient Greek performance provided by the link.  
The voice actors read it in a sort of emotionless, monotonous tone.  
Obviously, back when it was performed orally, the performers probably weren't professionally trained voice actors either, but the samples provided sounded no better than that Google Translate text-to-speech voice to me.  
It might've partially been due to the lack of background music, but for an oral performance, I expected a little bit more effort put into it, more *oomph*, more punch.  

> Second listening: ask what makes the speech feel patterned rather than like ordinary conversation.

If you compare the performed lines with natural human speech, the lines obviously feel more *patterned*, but compared to what I actually have in mind, they don't even hold a candle to normal human speech.  
I mentioned this in my Hamlet report: the actors in the play I watched performed Shakespeare's lines like natural human speech, when I expected something more "poetic" and "artsier". In the provided performances, the actors' delivery sits closer to human speech than something I expected from a **performance**.  
It's in the name: oral **performance**. I expected them to **perform**.

### The four lenses

#### Epic: What is worth remembering

If you compare the Odyssey to the masterpieces of our time, the story isn't really something incredibly transcendental.  
Much like what I conjectured in the Gilgamesh report, I believe this work was preserved and shared for the sole reason of being one of the best in its time.

Compared to Gilgamesh and the Iliad, the Odyssey feels more interesting to me in a way.  
For epics, the protagonist is expected to be a sort of "chosen one" by default, in contrast to genres like cyberpunk, where the protagonists are usually average joes, street thugs, pretty much all underdogs trying to survive.  
The Odyssey is no different.  
Odysseus is the protagonist of the epic because he is extraordinary.  

Though unlike Gilgamesh and Achilles, who were born with superhuman strength and abilities, Odysseus is a bit more appealing as his power is his wits.  
This is less obvious in the case of Achilles, but for Gilgamesh, he isn't a *hero* in the justice sense nor is he brave.  
Being brave means going into something knowing you might not come out in one piece.  
Gilgamesh does not do that.  
He has superhuman strength, superhuman durability, superhuman endurance: Things that help him survive whatever the story throws at him.  
But he didn't train for them; he didn't work for them.  
His "extraordinariness" was entirely the result of his birth, of him winning the genetic lottery.  
This is why Odysseus feels more appealing to me in this sense. Many of his wins feel more "earned" (that is, if you don't count all the times the gods helped him because he's their special little boy).  

#### Oral Tradition: What Does Repetition Do?

I think this is pretty obvious.  
It was performed orally, so repetition is a way to pace the audience, to make the wording less awkward.  
To make it feel "punchier", in a way.  
Repetition is much more forgiving orally than in text form, as in the case of those 13 winds in Gilgamesh.  

> “He struck the ground to confront him.  
> At their heels the earth split apart,  
> As they circled, the ranges of Lebanon were sundered!  
> The white clouds turned black,  
> Death rained down like fog upon them.  
> Shamash raised the great winds against Humbaba,  
> South wind, north wind, east wind, west wind, moaning wind,  
> Blasting wind, lashing wind, contrary wind, dust storm,  
> Demon wind, freezing wind, storm wind, whirlwind:  
> The thirteen winds blotted out Humbaba’s face,  
> He could not charge forward, he could not retreat.  
> Then Gilgamesh’s weapons defeated Humbaba.”  
> — Tablet V, 34-45

#### Storyteller and Audience

I used to play DnD with a group of people.  
I did sort of enjoy my time back then, and the Odyssey (much more than its prequel, the Iliad) feels very much like a DnD campaign.  
Probably because DnD gets its settings from these kinds of epics and mythology.  
Unlike DnD though, where the players get to build and write their own journey and battles, the Odyssey is more like what happens when a Dungeon Master goes off on a tangent of their own.  
It feels much more "lived-in" and better prepared than your average DnD campaign though, so it feels like a trade-off: Get a better story and world, or have a worse story that is much more personal and memorable.  

On second thought, could the Odyssey have been the result of the ancient equivalent of a DnD campaign? Like the best of them, transcribed into an epic?

### If we read the Odyssey only for its plot, what do we miss?

As said above, the Odyssey, when compared to modern acclaimed masterpieces (plot-wise), doesn't hold up as well (at least to me) when put alongside the heavyweights.  
Much like any media ever, consuming a piece of creative work only for the plot misses everything else it has to offer.  
Like what? The pacing, the poetry, the history, the voice, the performers' personalities?  
If one were to consume a piece of creative work only for the plot, it would be no different from reading the synopsis and summary on Wikipedia.
